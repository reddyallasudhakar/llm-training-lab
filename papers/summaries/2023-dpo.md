# Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model

[arXiv 2305.18290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290) · Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell et al. · May 2023 (NeurIPS 2023) · Lane: Foundations · Module: M15
*One-page summary written by Claude. Your own TLDR goes in `notes/`.*

## TLDR
RLHF's objective has a closed-form answer, so you can skip the reward model and the RL
loop. Training on preference pairs with one classification loss solves the same problem
as PPO-based RLHF, and it matched or beat PPO in the paper's tests.

## The problem
Classic RLHF has three stages: SFT, train a reward model on human comparisons, then run
PPO against that reward with a KL leash. The PPO stage samples from the model during
training, juggles several models, and is unstable and fiddly to tune.

## The mechanism: the derivation in 5 steps
Notation: `x` prompt, `y_w` the preferred answer, `y_l` the rejected one, `π_θ` the model
you train, `π_ref` the frozen SFT model, `r(x, y)` a reward, `σ` the sigmoid.
1. **The RLHF objective** (Eq. 3). Maximise reward, but stay close to the reference:
   `max_π  E[ r(x, y) ] − β · KL( π(y|x) || π_ref(y|x) )`.
   `β` sets how hard the leash pulls. Higher β keeps you nearer `π_ref`.
2. **Its optimal policy in closed form** (Eq. 4):
   `π*(y|x) = (1/Z(x)) · π_ref(y|x) · exp( r(x, y) / β )`.
   Reweight the reference by exponentiated reward. `Z(x)` sums this over every possible
   answer to normalise it, which is impossible to compute for text.
3. **Solve for the reward** (Eq. 5). Take logs and rearrange:
   `r(x, y) = β · log( π*(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) + β · log Z(x)`.
   Any reward can be written as a scaled log-ratio between a policy and the reference.
4. **Substitute into Bradley-Terry** (Eq. 1). Humans prefer `y_1` with probability
   `σ( r(x, y_1) − r(x, y_2) )`. It only uses the *difference* of rewards, and both
   answers share the same prompt, so `β · log Z(x)` cancels (Eq. 6).
5. **The DPO loss** (Eq. 7). Fit `π_θ` by maximum likelihood on the preference data:
   `L_DPO = − E[ log σ( β · log(π_θ(y_w|x) / π_ref(y_w|x)) − β · log(π_θ(y_l|x) / π_ref(y_l|x)) ) ]`.
   That is logistic regression where the "reward" is `r̂ = β · log(π_θ / π_ref)`. Your
   language model *is* the reward model.

**What the gradient does:** raise the log-probability of `y_w` and lower that of `y_l`,
with each pair weighted by `σ(r̂(y_l) − r̂(y_w))`. Pairs the model currently ranks the
wrong way get the biggest push. Without this weight, the model degenerated.

**In practice:** set `π_ref` to the SFT model that generated the pairs. If you don't have
it, SFT on the preferred answers first. Defaults: β = 0.1 (0.5 for TL;DR), batch 64,
RMSprop, learning rate 1e-6.

## Results
- **Sentiment (IMDb, GPT-2-large):** DPO had the best reward-vs-KL frontier, beating PPO
  even when PPO used the true reward.
- **Summarisation (TL;DR, GPT-J):** about 61% win rate vs reference summaries at
  temperature 0, against PPO's 57% at its best temperature. Humans preferred DPO samples
  over PPO samples 58% of the time.
- **Dialogue (Anthropic HH, Pythia-2.8B):** DPO was the only efficient method to beat the
  preferred answers in the test set, and roughly matched best-of-128 sampling.
- **Out of distribution (CNN/DailyMail):** win rate 0.36 vs PPO's 0.26 at temperature 0.
- GPT-4 judges agreed with humans about as often as humans agreed with each other.

## Limits
- Models only up to 6B, GPT-4 as the main judge, and the authors left open how reward
  over-optimisation shows up in DPO.
- **DPO overfits.** *IPO* (Azar et al., 2023) showed the KL leash weakens when
  preferences are near-deterministic, so the model drifts far from `π_ref`.
- **Well-tuned PPO can win.** *Is DPO Superior to PPO for LLM Alignment?* (Xu et al.,
  2024) found PPO ahead across tasks, and DPO can favour out-of-distribution answers.
- **It can exploit length** (*Park et al., 2024*), which motivated *SimPO* (Meng et al.,
  2024). It can also *lower* the chosen answer's probability, as long as the rejected one
  falls faster (*Smaug / DPOP*, Pal et al., 2024).

## Why it matters to you
At M15 you'll write this loss yourself; at M14 the reward model it replaces. Casefile
decision: build pairs where `y_w` is a correct claim audit and `y_l` is a plausible wrong
one (wrong exclusion clause, missed waiting period). Sample both from your own distilled
model and use that model as `π_ref`, since the derivation assumes the data came from
near `π_ref`. Watch the chosen answer's log-prob and the output length during training,
not just the loss.

## Words to know
**Bradley-Terry:** P(A beats B) = σ(score_A − score_B).
**Partition function Z(x):** the normaliser over all possible answers; it cancels here.
**Implicit reward:** `β · log(π_θ / π_ref)`, the reward your policy encodes.
**Reference model:** the frozen copy you measure drift against.
