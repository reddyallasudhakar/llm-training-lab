# On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes (GKD)

[arXiv 2306.13649](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649) · Agarwal, Vieillard et al. · Jun 2023 (ICLR 2024) · Lane: Foundations · Module: M12
*One-page summary written by Claude. Your own TLDR goes in `notes/`.*

## TLDR
Let the student write its own outputs, then have the teacher grade every token of them.
This fixes the gap between what the student trains on and what it sees when it
generates. It beat standard distillation on summarisation, translation and maths.

## The problem
Normal distillation trains the student on fixed sequences: ground truth, or text the
teacher wrote. At inference the student generates on its own, and one early wrong token
puts it in a prefix it never saw in training. Errors then compound. This is the
train/inference mismatch (also called exposure bias). A second issue: forward KL forces a
small student to spread probability over everything the teacher might say, including
tokens the teacher thinks are unlikely.

## The mechanism
**Token-level divergence** (Eq. 2): for input `x` and sequence `y` of length `L_y`,
`D(p_T || p_S)(y|x) = (1/L_y) · Σ_n D( p_T(· | y<n, x) || p_S(· | y<n, x) )`
- `p_T`, `p_S`: teacher and student next-token distributions over the whole vocabulary.
- `y<n`: the tokens so far. You compare full distributions at every position, not just
  the one sampled token.

**Which divergence `D`:**
- **Forward KL**, `KL(p_T || p_S)`: what supervised KD uses. It is mode-covering: the
  student is punished for missing anything the teacher might say.
- **Reverse KL**, `KL(p_S || p_T)`: mode-seeking. The student is punished for saying
  things the teacher wouldn't, so it focuses on the teacher's likely tokens and loses
  some diversity.
- **Generalised JSD(β)** (Eq. 1), with `M = βP + (1−β)Q`:
  `JSD(β)(P || Q) = β · KL(P || M) + (1−β) · KL(Q || M)`, with P = teacher, Q = student.
  Its gradients behave like forward KL as β → 0 and like reverse KL as β → 1. It stays
  bounded even when the two distributions don't overlap.

**The GKD objective:**
`L_GKD = (1−λ) · E_(x,y)~data [ D(p_T || p_S)(y|x) ] + λ · E_x, y~p_S [ D(p_T || p_S)(y|x) ]`
- `λ`, the **student data fraction**: the share of batches where `y` is sampled from the
  student itself (Algorithm 1 flips a coin with probability λ each step).
- λ = 0 with forward KL is ordinary supervised KD. λ = 1 is fully **on-policy** KD.
- No gradient flows through the sampling step. That keeps it close to supervised
  training: stable and cheap, unlike policy-gradient RL.
- Why on-policy fixes the mismatch: the student trains on the prefixes *it* produces,
  mistakes included, and the teacher shows it the right next-token distribution from
  there. The paper frames this as imitation learning with an interactive expert.
- **With RL** (Eq. 5): `(1−α) · reward − α · on-policy distillation loss`. You can
  maximise a reward while being pulled toward the teacher instead of the starting model.

## Results
- Teacher: SFT'd T5-XL (~3B). Students: T5-small (77M), base (250M), large (800M).
- Averaged over student sizes, on-policy GKD's gains over the starting student were
  **2.1× (summarisation), 1.7× (translation) and 1.9× (GSM8K)** those of baseline KD.
- On XSum, on-policy GKD trained on 5% of the data, with no ground-truth summaries, beat
  supervised KD and ImitKD trained on all of it.
- On GSM8K, using only student-generated chain-of-thought beat fixed or mixed data.
  Accuracy kept rising as the on-policy share went above 25%.
- **Best divergence depends on the task.** Forward KL did well on XSum (greedy) and
  GSM8K, JSD variants on translation, and reverse KL on FLAN instruction tuning (+2% BBH,
  +1% MMLU held-out).
- RL (entailment reward) plus GKD gave summaries more factually consistent than the teacher's.

## Limits
- Only T5 encoder-decoder models up to a 3B teacher, and no limitations section.
- Token-level losses need teacher and student to share a vocabulary. Cross-tokenizer
  methods such as *Universal Logit Distillation* (Boizard et al., 2024) came later.
- Every step costs a student generation plus a teacher forward pass. *DistiLLM* (Ko et
  al., 2024) cut this cost with a skewed KL and reused student samples.
- The idea held up and scaled. The *Qwen3* technical report (2025) uses on-policy
  distillation from large to small Qwen models, which is your exact setting.

## Why it matters to you
At M12 you'll use TRL's `GKDTrainer`, whose `lmbda` and `beta` are this paper's λ and β.
Casefile decision: teacher = your best 8B Qwen (or a larger Qwen on GCP), student = the
0.5B–3B model you want on the Mac. Same family means same tokenizer, which this method
needs. Start from your SFT checkpoint, as the paper does, and sweep λ ∈ {0.5, 1} and a
few β. Check which end of β TRL treats as forward KL before you read the sweep. Grade
results on your claim-audit eval, since the best divergence was task-dependent.

## Words to know
**On-policy:** trained on outputs the current model generated itself.
**Exposure bias:** a model trained only on correct prefixes fails on its own wrong ones.
**Mode-covering vs mode-seeking:** spread mass over everything vs commit to the main peaks.
**Student data fraction (λ):** the share of training sequences the student wrote.
